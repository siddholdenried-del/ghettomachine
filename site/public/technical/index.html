Inside the GhettoMachine

How our four-track groovebox works, from the key you press to the sound in your headphones.
4tracks
16patterns
64steps
16voices

GhettoMachine is BongWare custom firmware engineered by BongTech. It replaces the FM-1's stock firmware. It has one drum track (a 909 or an 808), three instrument tracks that can each be a mono synth, a poly synth or a sampler, a sequencer, effects, and a mastering chain. It's about 430 KB of code, and the same code runs in your browser, right here.

Historical tour (0.5). The specifications and test history below describe that build. The linked browser instrument now runs the newer development preview; use the current User Guide for its 128-step sequencer, 606 and track inserts.

This is the tour: what runs where, how a note becomes sound, where things are saved, and how we build and test it. Each chapter is coloured the way the FM-1's screen colours that part of the instrument.

Play it

IN THE BROWSER

This is GhettoMachine itself, compiled to WebAssembly: the same sequencer, drums, synth, effects and screen as on the FM-1, drawn as its panel. Press Switch on, then PLAY / STOP to hear its demo groove, and play it with the mouse or your computer's keyboard. Your work is kept in this browser. If an iPhone stays silent, turn up its volume, and turn off silent mode: older iOS versions mute web audio in it.

The box and the platform

HARDWARE

Inside the FM-1 is a JieLi WL82 chip: two processor cores, a floating-point unit that does single precision only, and 1 MB of flash memory. The screen is a 240 × 240 colour panel on a fast serial line. The front panel has 27 keys (16 white, 11 black), 14 buttons and 7 knobs: SELECT, ALGORITHM, PRESETS and K1 to K4.

We don't talk to that hardware directly. We build on fm1-base, a platform by Charles Vestal that grew out of the Felucca firmware. It gives us a small set of functions for the things every instrument needs: here are the buttons and keys, here's the screen, fill this audio buffer, save this file, send this MIDI. Our code is the app on top: one large file, app.c, for the instrument, plus a folder of sound engines.

Two loops: sound and everything else

TIMING

The most important idea in the whole program is that it runs as two separate loops that never wait for each other.

The audio loop

every 5.8 ms · 256 samples
  • Reads incoming MIDI
  • Moves the sequencer on, firing notes and drum hits exactly on time
  • Runs the LFOs and the arpeggiator
  • Renders the drums and the synth voices
  • Mixes, adds reverb and delay, runs the master bus
  • Hands 256 stereo samples to the codec

The main loop

up to 60 times a second
  • Reads buttons, keys and knobs
  • Changes the project when you edit something
  • Draws the screen
  • Saves to flash when you press SAVE
  • Lowers the voice count if the processor is too busy

The audio loop is an interrupt: when the codec needs more sound, the chip stops whatever it's doing and runs it. If it ever ran late you'd hear a click, so it does nothing slow. No drawing, no saving, no waiting. Everything slow happens in the main loop, in the time left over.

Both loops touch the same project, so when the main loop changes something the audio loop reads, like a step or a knob, it takes a short lock (it holds off the interrupt for a few microseconds) so the sound never sees half an edit.

The second core

The synth is the heaviest work. The 16 voices are split in two: one core renders half while the other renders the rest, and the halves are added together. They hand work back and forth every 32 samples (0.73 ms). Some FM-1 units have faults on the second core, so if it doesn't answer, everything runs on one core instead.

When the processor runs short

The main loop watches how busy the processor is. Above 85%, it takes a voice away from the pool every 40 ms; below 65%, it gives one back. You'd hear notes drop out of a big chord before you'd hear the audio break up. HOME shows the load as CPU, and SETUP turned with SELECT splits it by stage: the render reads a 24 MHz timer around the drums, the synth, the inserts and Color, the reverb and delay, the master bus and the rest, and keeps a running mean of each.

The 21 Nov CPU pass, measured in instructions on the Mac (scalar, as the FM-1 runs) over a busy scene: the 606 clap's core runs at 22.05 kHz with its 192-tap colour filters squeezed to 96 (they hold under 1% of their energy above 11 kHz) and its finishing stage moved there too, a third of its old cost; its hats and cymbal retune their 32 lines with a Taylor step instead of a sine each and sum plain and bell lines in separate loops; Color skips a DIGITAL too fine to reach a 16-bit output and works out WOW's two delays every 16 samples; the phaser computes its allpass coefficient every 16 samples instead of dividing twice a sample; the frequency shifter reads its Hilbert window without wrapping. A track's insert and Color sleep once 4,096 samples of silence have gone in and come out. The busy scene costs about a fifth less; a stopped one, two-thirds less.

The sequencer

STEPS

A pattern holds all four tracks. There are 16 patterns. Each track's part of a pattern has its own length (1 to 64 steps) and its own rate (1/16, 1/8, 1/32 or triplets), so a 12-step bassline can run against 16-step drums and drift in and out of phase. That's called polymeter.

A drum step

  • One bit per pad: hit or no hit
  • An accent bit across the kit
  • Optional: a chance (100% down to 5%)
  • Optional: locks, a knob's value for that step only

An instrument step

  • Up to 4 notes (a chord)
  • Velocity, 1 to 127
  • Gate, in eighths of a step, up to almost 32 steps
  • Chance, and locks for any sound knob

Inside the audio loop, each track keeps a countdown in samples to its next step. Every 256-sample block is cut into pieces at exactly the samples where a step or a bar begins, so a hit lands on its sample, not on the next block. SWING (50 to 75%) pushes every second step later.

The bar

The start of a bar is where things change. A pattern you picked while playing waits for it (the band shows >02), then every track restarts at step 1. The song chain moves to its next entry there. LFOs set to restart each bar do so. The metronome's first beat is the bar's start.

Undo

Before an edit to a pattern, the sequencer takes a copy of that pattern. EDIT + OCT− puts the copy back; EDIT + OCT+ redoes. It keeps 8 copies. Edits of the same kind close together, like several turns of one knob, share one copy.

The drums

909 · 808

The drum sounds come from X0X, another FM-1 firmware: its models of the 909 (after 9W9) and the 808 (after 8W8). They're synthesised the way the originals worked: oscillators, noise and envelopes, with a few short recorded samples for the 909's cymbals and hats. Both kits share one layout of 11 pads, so a beat keeps its meaning when you switch kits:

Pad1234567891011
909BDSDLTMTHTRSCPCHOHCRRD
808BDSDLTMTHTRSCPCHOHCYCB

Every pad has its own knobs (tune, tone, decay and so on) and its own reverb and delay sends. A tail keeps ringing when you change kits mid-note, because the other kit keeps rendering until it's quiet.

The two kits play at the same level. 8W8 trimmed its 808 sounds for its own master drive and glue, which this firmware leaves to its master bus, so out of the box the 808's drums came out far under the 909: the kick 17.7 dB, the snare and toms 14–16 dB, while its hats were nearly level. A whole groove played 12 dB quieter. Since 21 Nov each 808 sound gets a make-up gain after its drive and distortion, bringing it to the loudness of the 909 pad that plays its part (BS.1770 momentary loudness of a single hit at default settings): every pad now lands within 0.2 dB, the cowbell a little under the ride. Saved kits and projects keep their knobs and simply play louder; the drive and distortion sound as they did.

The drums' LFO is a trick on top of the locks: at each hit of the pad you picked, it writes a new value into one of that pad's knobs, then puts the knob back. That's how sample-and-hold on the closed hat's decay makes every hat different.

The kick also feeds the master bus's PUMP: the sequencer tells the compressor a kick is coming, and it ducks the whole mix, the sidechain pump of house music without needing to listen for it.

The synth and the sampler

T2 · T3 · T4

Each instrument track is MONO, POLY or SAMPLER. All three use the same voice: a sound source, then a filter, then an amplifier, each with its own envelope. Only the source changes.

SourceTwo oscillators (saw, square, triangle, sine, PWM, sync, sub), a sub oscillator and noise. Or, on a SAMPLER track, the sample.
FilterLow-, band- or high-pass, with resonance, moved by its own envelope, by the key, and by the LFO.
AmplifierAttack, decay, sustain, release. Velocity sets how loud.
The track's mixLevel, pan, and its reverb and delay sends.

The synth engine is Felucca-Engines' ANALOG voice. It works in whole numbers rather than floating point, which is fast on this chip, and it updates its slower controls (envelopes, the filter's setting) once every 32 samples instead of every sample. We added a sub oscillator and a second oscillator you can tune in intervals.

Sixteen voices for three tracks

The three tracks share one pool of 16 voices. POLY takes a voice per note, up to 8. MONO uses one voice, or up to 4 stacked and detuned (unison), and remembers which keys are still held, so letting go of a note falls back to the one underneath. When the pool is full, a new note takes a voice from one already sounding (voice stealing).

The sampler

Samples live in flash, not in memory, and they're played straight out of flash while the sound runs. To fit more in, each sample is stored at 12 bits: two samples packed into three bytes. That gives about 3.3 seconds at 44.1 kHz shared by the three tracks, or 6.6 seconds at 22 kHz.

A recording (hold REC, press SEL) goes into a 132 KB buffer in memory first: up to 2 seconds of the FM-1's own mix. When it's kept, it's written into the sample bank, with a small header written last. If the power goes mid-write, the bank still holds a complete old sample rather than half a new one. That same buffer is the delay's memory the rest of the time, which is why the delay pauses while you sample.

A deeper synth

0.3

The synth update added two things to the ANALOG voice: more character in the sound itself, and much more movement. A sound went from 26 settings to 64.

OSC 1 and OSC 2OSC 2 can have its own wave. FM: OSC 2 bends OSC 1's phase. RING: the two multiplied. DRIFT: each voice wanders a few cents.
Shaper (PRE)Fold, clip, rectify or crush, with a bias.
Drive and filterAs before.
Shaper (POST)Or here, after the filter: crush is the classic.
AmplifierIts envelope, velocity, and the matrix's LEVEL.

FM, ring and the shaper

FM here is phase modulation, the way Yamaha's DX synths did it: OSC 2's output nudges where OSC 1 is in its cycle, which adds harmonics without changing the pitch. INTERVAL sets the ratio between the two (+12 is 2:1, odd intervals sound clangy and bell-like). The amount is squared, so the low end of the knob is fine-grained, and the top notes get less, because FM at high pitches makes frequencies above what 44.1 kHz can hold and they fold back as grit.

The wavefolder comes from Felucca's PHASE engine: the wave goes through a sine, so a loud peak folds back down instead of clipping, and more gain means more folds. It's the West Coast synth sound. A small filter after it removes the slow offset that folding a lopsided wave creates.

The matrix

Before, each track had one LFO that could move one thing. Now each sound has a matrix of six slots, each one "this source moves that destination by this much". Sources are the LFOs, the three envelopes, velocity, the note, a random value per note and four macro knobs; destinations are nearly every knob of the voice.

The matrix is worked out for each voice separately, 1,378 times a second (every 32 samples). That's what makes per-note things possible: the third envelope (MOD ENV) and LFO 2 belong to the note, not the track, so a chord's notes each shimmer on their own. Working it out is cheap, a few multiplications per slot. When the matrix moves something the engine reads, like FM or resonance, that voice gets its own copy of the settings for the next 32 samples.

Pitch needed one trick. The engine's pitch steps are 1/16 of a semitone, too coarse for a gentle vibrato. The matrix's pitch goes through the voice's fine tune as well, the same path unison detune uses, so it moves smoothly.

Staying the same when unused

Every new feature is switched off by a zero: FM 0, RING 0, AMOUNT 0, an empty matrix slot. When they're all off, the voice runs exactly the code it ran before. We checked: 0.2's 15 factory sounds, rendered by the old synth and the new one, come out byte for byte identical. When a feature is on, the voice runs a second, richer loop; on the Mac that costs about 1.5 times a plain voice.

The testing found two old integer overflows (a very loud unison voice could overflow its pan multiply, and the sampler shifted a negative number). Both fixed, without changing any sound.

The 303 generator

0.4

Roland's TB-303 bass line owes as much of its sound to its sequencer as to its synth. Every step is a short note, a note held on into the next, or a slide that glides into the next pitch without starting again, and some notes are accented: louder, with the filter thrown open for a moment. The ACID pages (SEQ, after STEPS, on T2–T4) write lines like that into a track's part, from a handful of knobs.

Seven dice a stepFrom the line's seed: does it play, which note, an octave, which way, a slide, an accent, half or whole. MUTATE rolls a few steps again.
The knobsSet how many dice come up: DENSITY, WHOLE, SLIDE, ACCENT, OCTAVE. ROOT, SCALE and NOTES choose the notes.
Ordinary stepsA note, a gate (half a step, held to the next note, or past it) and two marks: slide and accent.
Any soundThe sequencer hands the marks to the synth: a slide glides the held voice, an accent is louder and brighter.

Dice that stay put

Each step's dice come from a hash of the seed and the step's number, so they never change unless you ask for a new line (OCT+) or a mutation (OCT−). The knobs only move the line each die has to beat. Turn DENSITY up and more steps play while the ones already there stay; turn any knob back and you get exactly the line you had. The root is three times as likely as any other note, and NOTES picks a bass player's favourites first: root, fifth, third, seventh.

Half, whole and slide

A 303's note lasts half a step. A whole note is held until just before the next note, across any rests, which is a 303's tie. A slide holds the note past the start of the next step, so the next note arrives while it's still sounding and glides in from its pitch. On STEPS you can set any step by hand: hold it, press OCT+ for HALF, WHOLE or SLIDE, and turn VELOCITY past 127 for an accent.

On any sound

The two marks live in bits each step already had spare (a velocity only needs 7 bits, a chance too), so the save format didn't change for them, and nothing saved before has them set. The sequencer passes them to the synth with each note. A slide takes the voice that's still holding the last note, whatever kind of sound it is (a mono bass, a poly pad, a sample), and moves its pitch over 58 ms, or the sound's own GLIDE time. An accent makes the note 1.4 times louder and opens the filter by about an octave, falling back over 200 ms, which is roughly what the 303's accent circuit does.

An unmarked note takes exactly the old path: 0.2's factory sounds still render byte for byte the same.

LFOs, arpeggiator, chords, locks

MOVEMENT

These all run inside the audio loop, block by block, so they stay in time with the sequencer.

LFO

Every track has one: sine, triangle, ramps, square, sample-and-hold or smooth random, free or synced to the tempo (4 bars down to 1/32), restarting freely, on each note, or each bar. On T2 to T4 it moves one thing: cutoff, pitch, level, pulse width, resonance, detune, mix, drive, noise, sub, or a sample's start. It's also a source in the matrix (above), alongside LFO 2, which each note has its own of.

Arpeggiator

It collects the notes you hold (and the sequencer's chords) and plays them one at a time: up, down, both, random or as played, over 1 to 4 octaves, at a note value you pick. LATCH keeps them going after you let go.

Chord mode

A key plays a chord built on it: one of 12 shapes, from major and minor to sevenths, sus chords and power chords, inverted up to three times. The chord is stored as a list of intervals; the key you pressed is remembered with the chord it started, so it lets go of the same notes even if you change the chord while holding it. Chords go into the arpeggiator like held notes, so one key can play an arpeggiated chord.

Locks

A lock is a knob's value for one step. It's stored as a small list per part (step, which knob, value) rather than a copy of every knob on every step, which keeps a pattern small. When the step plays, its locks are applied just before the note, and the knob goes back to its own value at the next step.

Recording

HOLD REC

With REC on and the pattern playing, a note goes to the nearest point of the grid you chose: a step, or every 2 or 4 steps. Its gate is how long you held the key. If the nearest point is just ahead, the sequencer is told to skip it once, so you don't hear it twice. With the pattern stopped, on the KEYS page, notes go in one step at a time.

The metronome counts quarters of the bar. It's synced to the sequencer at each bar's start and counts the other three beats itself. Each click is a short sine burst, 1.5 kHz on beat 1 and 1 kHz on the rest, made by a two-line recurrence (no sine table needed). It's added after the master bus, so it's never in a sample you record or the loudness meter (it is in the USB master pair).

The count-in is a countdown in samples. The audio loop clicks the beats and starts the sequencer in the block where the countdown runs out. A note you play in the last half beat is put on step 1, and step 1 is skipped once at the start so you don't hear it twice.

QUANTIZE moves every note of the part to the nearest point of the grid, merging notes that meet into a chord and moving their locks with them. LEGATO sets each note's gate to reach the next note, plus one eighth of a step of overlap, so a MONO sound with LEGATO on ties them together. Both take an undo copy first.

Playing live

HOME

HOME is a scene grid: 16 patterns across, a track a row. Under it is one small change to the sequencer: each track has its own pointer to the pattern it plays, its clip. A scene sets all four pointers; a clip sets one. Everything that edits (steps, notes, locks, undo) follows the pointer, so you always edit what you hear.

Changes wait for a boundary. The audio loop already cuts its blocks where a bar starts, so a waiting clip or scene starts there, on its sample. With LAUNCH at BEAT it also cuts at quarter bars; at NOW the main loop switches at once and restarts the bar from there. A scene's follow action is a counter of bars: when it reaches the number you set, the next scene is queued as if you'd pressed it.

Perform keys

The main loop only sets a bit for each perform key held. The audio loop compares the bits with what it saw last block and switches the effect on or off at the block's start.

  • ROLL keeps the sequencer counting silently and runs a second countdown that replays each track's step at the roll's rate. Let go and the pattern is where it would have been.
  • STUTTER and TAPE STOP record the output into the delay's 132 KB memory, lent to them while held. STUTTER loops a slice; TAPE STOP reads it back slower and slower.
  • FILTER, THROW and WASH borrow the DJ filter, the delay and the reverb: they set their knobs while held and put them back after.
  • CRUSH holds each sample for several and rounds it to fewer bits.
  • FILL points the drums at the next pattern for as long as it's held.

Sounds per pattern

When a track moves to another pattern, its sound (a few dozen bytes of knob values) is copied into the pattern it leaves, and the new pattern's sound is copied out and handed to the synth. It's small enough to happen inside the audio loop at the bar, with no gap. The drums store just which kit.

Following a MIDI clock

A MIDI clock is 24 pulses a beat. The FM-1 reads MIDI once a block, so it only knows a pulse came within some 5.8 ms. Instead of trusting single gaps, it notes the time of every 6th pulse (a 16th note) and measures the tempo across the last four beats, where 5.8 ms is a fraction of a percent. Then at every 16th it compares where its own sequencer is with where the clock says it should be, and shifts the whole groove an eighth of the way there (a quarter if it's more than 10 ms off). That's the same shift as NUDGE. So the two can't drift apart, and the block's uncertainty shrinks to a millisecond or two.

Building this turned up an older bug. When the tempo changed mid-bar, each track took the new tempo at its next step, but the bar's countdown kept the old one until the bar ended, so step 1 and the bar could slip apart. Now any tempo change stretches every countdown together.

The project file's last bytes

The new settings were added at the end of the project. A project saved before them reads in shorter, and the missing end is filled with zeros, which mean "off". The project now uses 32,680 of its 32,736 bytes, so the next big addition will need a second file.

Mixer and effects

SENDS

Each track has a level, a pan and two sends. The sends are added into two buses, one for the reverb and one for the delay, and what comes back is added to the dry mix.

Reverb

  • Four diffusers smear the sound
  • Then an 8-line feedback network makes the room
  • DECAY, TONE, LOW CUT, LEVEL

Delay

  • Time in note values, following the tempo
  • DIGI (clean) or TAPE (warmer, with wear)
  • Ping-pong between the sides by WIDTH

The reverb and the delay are from X0X. The delay's 1.5 seconds of memory is the sampler's recording buffer, lent back and forth. The DJ filter on the FX pages sits on the master bus: left of centre it's a low-pass that closes, right of centre a high-pass that opens.

The master bus

MASTERING

Everything you hear goes through one chain, in this order:

Trim, −14 dBThe tracks add up to 11–13 dB over full scale. Turning the whole mix down here gives the chain headroom.
EQLow cut, low shelf, mid bell, high shelf. Standard biquad filters; a band at 0 dB is skipped entirely.
Compressor + PUMPReads the level over about 8 ms, works out the reduction in dB with a soft knee, and smooths it. PUMP ducks at each kick.
DJ filterThe FX pages' sweep.
TapeA soft curve (tanh) that leaves quiet sound alone and rounds the peaks; WARMTH adds even harmonics; TONE rolls off the top.
StereoMid and side: WIDTH scales the side, BASS MONO removes the side below a frequency.
Gain, then the limiterLooks 64 samples (1.5 ms) ahead, so it turns down before a peak arrives; aims 0.5 dB under the CEILING; a soft knee catches the rest.
Meters, the click, then MASTERThe loudness meter reads here. USB takes its copy before the volume knob, which comes last, so the knob never changes what the chain does or what a computer records.

The loudness meter follows the broadcast standard (ITU-R BS.1770): it filters the sound the way the ear weighs it, averages the power over 3 seconds (and 0.4 for M), and shows LUFS. A 1 kHz tone at −20 dBFS on both sides reads −20.0, which is how we checked it.

The seven presets set the chain at once, and they're level-matched by measurement: on the demo groove, bypassed reads −15.1 LUFS and CLEAN −15.2, so switching compares the sound, not the volume. LOUD reaches −9.

Stems over USB

0.5

Over its USB cable the FM-1 is also a sound card that needs no driver. fm1-base provides it: the audio input carries what the FM-1 plays, and the output lets the computer play through the FM-1. In 0.5 the input has ten channels: the master on 1–2, then each track alone, the drums on 3–4 and T2, T3, T4 on 5–6, 7–8 and 9–10. A DAW records all five pairs at once.

Ten is the most there's room for. The FM-1's USB runs at full speed, where an audio packet can be at most 1,023 bytes, sent once a millisecond. At 44.1 kHz a packet holds up to 46 frames; 46 frames of ten 16-bit channels is 920 bytes. Twelve channels, or 24 bits, wouldn't fit. A 48 kHz stream would mean resampling ten channels, which costs more than it's worth.

Each stem is taken where its track joins the mix: after its level and pan (and so after mutes and solo), before the reverb, the delay and the master bus. The drums are copied out of the mix buffer before the synth adds into it; the synth already sums each track's voices on their own before mixing them, so it keeps that sum for each track. The samples go straight into fm1-base's USB ring as each block renders, beside the master's, so the five pairs stay lined up to the sample. When no computer is recording, the ring gives nothing back and the app skips the work.

The stems sit at the level the screen's track meters show, which is the mix after the −14 dB trim, as the master bus receives it. The four add up to the master bus's input without the reverb and delay returns, and the demos peak 6 to 14 dB under full scale. They have no limiter, so a track turned up far enough clips in its stem. The metronome is on the master pair: fm1-base copies the master from what the app renders, and the click is already in that.

The MASTER knob is in neither. The app renders at full level, fm1-base copies that for USB, and only then turns it down by MASTER for the speaker and headphones. So the tracks' own levels set the USB mix, and you can listen quietly, or not at all, while a computer records.

The first recording from the FM-1 itself clicked about a dozen times in 9 seconds: whole frames held for up to 6 ms, then a jump. fm1-base's USB ring is filled when a render finishes, and with only a few milliseconds in hand, a render that ran long left it empty, so the stream repeated its last frame. It now keeps a whole extra block in hand, enough for a render to use all of its time, at the cost of 5.8 ms more latency. Recorded again on 21 Nov, with MASTER at 0: no frames held, and the master pair at its full level. The ring moved into the pool too, so 0.5 uses slightly less fixed RAM than 0.4.

The screen

240 × 240

the page's picture

Every page has three parts. The dark band on top says where you are and shows the output meters; turn a knob and it shows the change large for a moment. The page in the middle draws what you're editing: steps, a filter curve, an envelope, a sample. The knob strip at the bottom is four cards in the same order as K1 to K4.

Since 0.5 the screen follows Bong Cult's Dungeon BBS look: a near-black canvas, bone text, 1-pixel rules and square corners, the knob cards in one ruled grid. Violet marks what is selected (the knob being turned, the page you are on, the pad, slot or entry picked), pale violet a lock, and acid green only live readings, the meters. The colour is still the section's, for its name and what it draws: 909 orange, 808 blue, T2 green, T3 purple, T4 yellow, HOME and the mixer bone, FX cyan, the master bus rose, recording red. The labels are Barlow Semi Condensed; the counters and the line under the title are IBM Plex Mono. Both are compact bitmap fonts at build time.

Why the meters don't repaint everything

Sending a full screen down the serial line takes about 21 ms, longer than a frame. So the screen redraws fully only when something changes. The meters redraw about 30 times a second, and only the band (about 4 ms) plus the page if it has moving parts. The knob cards wait for a knob to turn.

The page's drawing code can't use the maths library the sound engines use (their names clash with the platform's), so the curves get their sine, log and tanh from a small separate unit.

Where things are saved

1 MB FLASH

The FM-1 has no file system for us; it has 1 MB of flash with fixed areas. To scale:

Boot code (M-VAVE's, never written)0x00000 · 16 KB
The firmware: our 425 KB, room to grow0x04000 · 572 KB
The project, two copies0x97000 · 2 × 32 KB
The sample bank0xA7000 · 212 KB
Panel calibration, Bluetooth pairing0xDC000 · 16 KB
Your presets: 16 sounds, 8 kits0xFC000 · 12 KB
Platform and spare areasthe rest

The project (patterns, song, sounds, mixer, effects, master bus, LFOs, arps, recording settings) is saved when you press SAVE, in one of two copies, alternately, each with a checksum. If the power goes during a save, the other copy is still whole and loads instead. A project file must be exactly the size a version expects. Small additions went into spare bytes or onto the end, which is why saved projects survived the earlier updates. The synth update needed more room than that (38 new settings for each of 51 sounds), so it has a new format: each step gave up a spare byte it never used (3 KB in all), which paid for the bigger sounds with 1.2 KB to spare. A file in the old format is read a piece at a time straight from flash and rearranged as it loads, with the new settings at values that sound the same. The first SAVE writes the new format. 0.4 put the 303 generator's settings onto the end again (format 3), and a project saved by 0.3 loads as it was.

Samples are kept the moment they're recorded or sent from the Mac. Presets are kept the moment you save one. Neither waits for SAVE.

Flash can only be erased 4 KB at a time and wears out after many erases, which is part of why saving is a button rather than automatic.

Building, testing, installing

ON THE MAC

One program, three places to run it

The same code builds three ways. For the FM-1, a JieLi compiler (running in Docker) makes the firmware image. For the Mac, an ordinary compiler builds a simulator: the same app, with fm1-base faking the buttons, keys, screen and audio. The simulator even avoids the one floating-point instruction the FM-1 lacks, so it computes the same samples. And Emscripten compiles it to WebAssembly for the emulator at the top of this page, its sound made in an audio worklet.

The tests

The simulator runs scripts that press buttons, turn knobs, play keys and send MIDI, wait, and then check what happened through hooks in the app: which step a note landed on, a gate's length, the output's peak level, how many dB the compressor took off.

Installing

The build ends in a .fwsc package with an identity, FM-1_800. Installing it over USB works because the firmware already on the FM-1 (X0X the first time, GhettoMachine since) includes an updater. The Mac sends the package over MIDI; the FM-1 checks it, restarts into a small loader, compares the flash 4 KB at a time and rewrites only what changed, then starts the new firmware.

The ESP32-S3 board is the rescue route. When an install is cut off and the FM-1 won't start, the S3 talks to the chip's built-in boot mode directly: it can read the whole flash for a backup and write the firmware area back. That's how the FM-1 came back on 18 November.

Not measured yet on the FM-1 itself: how busy the processor gets with all 16 voices, a dense 808 and every effect on. The voice limiter is there for that.

Words used here

GLOSSARY
Block
The 256 samples (5.8 ms) the audio loop makes each time it runs.
Interrupt
The chip stopping what it's doing to run something urgent, here, making the next block of sound.
Gate
How long a note is held, measured in steps.
Lock
A knob's value for one step only.
Polymeter
Tracks of different lengths looping against each other.
Voice
One sounding note's worth of synth: oscillators, filter and amplifier. There are 16.
Clip
The pattern one track is playing, chosen on the scene grid. A scene puts every track on the same pattern.
FM
Frequency (here phase) modulation: one oscillator wobbling another so fast it changes the tone, not the pitch.
Wavefolder
A shaper that folds a wave's peaks back down instead of cutting them off, adding bright harmonics.
Mod matrix
A table of "this moves that by this much", so any source can move any knob.
Macro
One knob that moves several settings at once, through the matrix.
MIDI clock
24 pulses a beat sent down a MIDI cable, so two machines keep the same tempo.
Biquad
The standard small digital filter, the building block of the EQ and the loudness meter.
Limiter
A very fast compressor that stops the output passing a ceiling.
LUFS
Loudness as the ear hears it, averaged over time. Streaming services aim near −14.
dBFS
Level against the loudest sample possible, which is 0.
Flash
The memory that keeps its contents with the power off: the firmware, projects, samples.
Simulator
The firmware built to run on the Mac, used to test without the FM-1.
Slide
A 303 note held into the next one, which glides in from its pitch without a new attack.
Accent
A 303 note played louder, with the filter opened for a moment.
WebAssembly
A compact program format browsers run at close to native speed: how the emulator runs the firmware.
